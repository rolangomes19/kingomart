# UX and behaviour evidence for "short banner on top, products near or above the fold" (ecommerce and marketplace homepages)

Current as of 14 Oct 2026. Evidence tags: **[STRONG]** = named research firm, stated method, large sample. **[MEDIUM]** = named source, method partly stated or older. **[WEAK]** = vendor blog, one A/B test, no sample, or unverified. **[OLD]** = before 2020, shown with date.

Headline for the writer:
- The fold still matters. Attention drops fast below it. Best data: NN/g 2018.
- No source I found tests "short banner plus products" against "tall hero" head to head.
- No source I found shows Amazon, Zalando, bol, Etsy or ASOS moved products up because it beat promo content. The user's assumption lacks support in published evidence. Say so plainly.
- Carousel click data is mostly old (2013) and from universities, not shops. Direction is consistent: the first slide gets most clicks, total clicks are low.
- B2B "search and reorder beat banners" has no hard behavioural study I could find. Only vendor surveys.

## 1. What do NN/g, Baymard, Contentsquare, Hotjar, Google and academic eye tracking say about above-the-fold attention and scroll depth?

### Takeaway
NN/g eye tracking (2018) shows 57% of viewing time is above the fold and 74% falls in the first two screenfuls. The fold is softer than in 2010 (80%) but attention still falls sharply after it. Contentsquare, Hotjar and Google gave no usable scroll-depth numbers in what I could reach.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG, 2018]** NN/g: over 130,000 eye fixations from 120 participants, 1920x1080 screens, tasks across news, ecommerce, blogs, FAQs, encyclopedic pages and search result pages. Above the fold = 57% of viewing time (80% in the 2010 study). First two screenfuls (to 2160 px) = 74%. Second screenful alone = about 17%. First three screenfuls = 81%. Top 20% of page = over 42% of time. Top 40% = over 65%. — [NN/g, Scrolling and Attention, Apr 2018](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)
- **[STRONG, 2018]** NN/g author quotes: "the pattern of a sharp decrease in attention following the fold remains the same in 2018 as in 2010." "People still don't scroll a lot." Limit: figures include short pages and users who gave up early, so they do not isolate scroll depth on long pages. The study is not homepage-only. — [NN/g, Scrolling and Attention](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)
- **[MEDIUM, 2015, OLD]** NN/g: across 57,453 fixations, the 100 px just above the fold got 102% more views than the 100 px just below. A Google ad study cited by NN/g: ad viewability 73% above the fold, 44% below (a 66% drop). NN/g's mid-point: 84% average difference above vs below. — [NN/g, Page Fold Manifesto, 1 Feb 2015](https://nngroup.com/articles/page-fold-manifesto)
- **[MEDIUM, 2015, OLD]** NN/g claims the fold exists on every device, only its position varies, and users scroll "when there is reason to." Visible teasers of content below the fold raise scrolling. — [NN/g, Page Fold Manifesto](https://nngroup.com/articles/page-fold-manifesto)
- **[MEDIUM, 2025]** Baymard survey of 1,167 US online shoppers: 33% would start by scrolling the homepage when casually browsing a visually driven product, more than any other strategy. — [Baymard, Ecommerce Homepage UX](https://baymard.com/research-articles/e-commerce-home-page-focus)
- **[MEDIUM, 2025]** Contentsquare 2025 Digital Experience Benchmarks: billions of sessions, 9 industries, 6,000 websites. Trends listed: conversions -6.1%, bounces up, content consumption -6.5%. No scroll-rate figure on the page. — [Contentsquare benchmark insights](https://go.contentsquare.com/en/benchmark-insights)
- **[WEAK]** Contentsquare scroll-map guide (updated Feb 2026) gives no scroll or fold statistics. One case: De Beers added a sticky CTA after seeing users scroll, and "conversions increased 10%." No year, no sample, vendor content. — [Contentsquare, Scroll maps guide](https://contentsquare.com/guides/heatmaps/scroll-maps/)
- **[WEAK, 2016, OLD]** Contentsquare (French blog): "users scroll 2x faster on mobile than on tablet." Says six months of millions of sessions. No sample, no source detail. Page is a 2016 post updated 2023. — [Contentsquare FR](https://contentsquare.com/fr-fr/blog/ux-mobile-scroll/)

### Inferences
- NN/g 2018 is the best single data point for "how much of the first impression is the first screen." Roughly half of viewing time sits above the fold, three quarters in the first two screens. Content in the first two screens gets most of the attention.
- A 120-person, 1080p lab study is not a homepage conversion test. It shows attention, not buying.
- The "2010 to 2018" shift (80% to 57%) means the fold is less absolute but "top of page wins" still holds.

### Gaps
- No Hotjar scroll-depth benchmark with sample size found. Search only returned vendor explainers.
- No Google / Think with Google scroll or fold dataset found in the time spent.
- No Contentsquare scroll-rate benchmark number found (the 2025 and 2026 report PDFs were not reachable). Try the full benchmark download.
- NN/g has not published a 2020 to 2026 scroll study that I found. 2018 is the newest.

## 2. Do homepage carousels and large hero banners get clicks? What do tests show?

### Takeaway
Carousel click rates are low and the first slide takes most of them. Best-known numbers are from 2013 university sites, not shops. Baymard (2025 update) says most users will not see all slides and 46% of carousels have usability faults. NN/g says users often scroll past large carousel images.

### Cited Findings
- **[OLD 2013, MEDIUM]** Erik Runyon, ND.edu and 4 other sites, 1 Jan to 30 Jun 2013, observational, no stats tests. ND.edu: 3,755,297 homepage visits, 1.07% clicked a feature, 89.1% of those clicks on position 1 and 3.1% on position 2. Four smaller sites: 1.27% to 9.41% clicked a feature; position 1 took 54.6% to 84.8% of clicks. Only the news site with an auto-playing carousel had the highest rate (9.41%, 43,724 visits). Sites were universities, not shops. — [Erik Runyon, Carousel Interaction Stats](https://erikrunyon.com/2013/07/carousel-interaction-stats/)
- **[OLD 2013, WEAK]** Econsultancy (29 Jan 2013): in the Notre Dame study, all five slides drew clicks from about 1% of visitors, first slide 84% of clicks. Dan Barker tracked carousels where moving from a static spot to a carousel got fewer clicks than the static spot. First slide won even when later slides had stronger offers. No sample sizes or dates given for Barker. About 50% of the top 20 UK retailers used carousels then. — [Econsultancy](https://econsultancy.com/carousels-on-ecommerce-sites-are-they-worth-bothering-with/)
- **[MEDIUM, updated Apr 2025]** Baymard: 33% of top US and European ecommerce desktop sites have a homepage carousel. 46% of carousels have usability issues. "Most users won't see all the slides in a homepage carousel, even if it autorotates." Suggested rotation: 5 to 7 s for slides with a header and few tags; up to 10 s for text-heavy slides. No sample size stated for the carousel claims. — [Baymard, Homepage carousel](https://baymard.com/blog/homepage-carousel)
- **[MEDIUM, reviewed Aug 2026]** NN/g: people "often immediately scroll past these large images" and miss content after frame 1. Animated ads were viewed 27% of the time in NN/g eye tracking. Keep to 5 frames or fewer. Avoid auto-forward on mobile. A static hero image may work better. No click-rate figure given. — [NN/g, Carousel usability](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/designing-effective-carousels/)
- **[MEDIUM, 2015]** Smashing Magazine (9 Feb 2015): about 7.5 million carousel interaction events across several mid-sized to large ecommerce sites (each over $20M sales a year), over 11 months. 15.7% of users interacted with slide 2 directly. Scope looks like product image carousels (thumbnails, zoom), not homepage promo banners. The fetched summary did not state this, so treat scope as unclear. — [Smashing Magazine](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2015/02/carousel-usage-exploration-on-mobile-e-commerce-websites)
- **[WEAK, 2019]** Booking.com tile test: control showed 5 location tiles at once, variant showed 3 at a time in a user-controlled carousel. GoodUI (not Booking) read the result as the carousel losing. No numbers. Screenshots dated 25 Sep 2019. — [GoodUI](https://goodui.org/leaks/bookings-carousel-fails-in-this-experiment-as-predicted/)
- **[WEAK, 2026]** Optimizely field notes (12 Jan 2026), retailer not named, no sample size: a homepage carousel that promoted a wide range of items including full outfits lowered add-to-cart by 6.07% and click-through by 10.4%. — [Optimizely](https://www.optimizely.com/field-notes/articles/experimentation-use-cases-retail-homepage)
- **[WEAK, A/B, undated]** Blend Commerce, unnamed apparel brand, 2-week test: a simpler hero (story widgets removed, bullet value points, "4,000+ happy customers" line, two CTAs) gave desktop conversion +26%, revenue per user +27%, add-to-cart +8.27%. Mobile conversion fell, so it stayed on desktop only. Sample size not stated. Vendor blog, one test. — [Blend Commerce](https://blendcommerce.com/blogs/ab-tests-shopify/above-the-fold-homepage-optimisations)

### Inferences
- Two lines hold across 2013 to 2026 sources: first slide takes most clicks; total carousel clicks are low. Hero tests only show that a clearer hero beats a cluttered one, not that a hero beats products.
- Click rates of about 1% come from university homepages. Do not quote them as ecommerce results.
- Erik Runyon's own page says 89.1% for ND.edu position 1; Econsultancy says 84%. Different periods. Use "about 85 to 90%."

### Gaps
- No Baymard or NN/g published click-rate figure for ecommerce homepage carousels.
- No Conversion Rate Experts public test found for hero vs product-first.
- No 2020 to 2026 independent carousel click-rate study on shops found.

## 3. Does a product-first homepage convert better than a promo-first one? Any marketplace case study?

### Takeaway
I found no published test or case study from Amazon, Zalando, bol, Etsy, ASOS or Booking that shows product-first beating promo-first. The user's assumption (top marketplaces put products near the fold to favour buying over marketing) has no support in the evidence I could reach. What exists: Baymard says users judge catalog range from the homepage, and a few vendor tests favour product-led or personalised blocks.

### Cited Findings
- **[MEDIUM, updated Apr 2025]** Baymard: first-time users use the homepage and main navigation to judge site type and range. 22% of ecommerce sites show an inadequate product range on the homepage (benchmark, no sample stated). Show at least 40% of product types (Key Takeaways say 40 to 50%; the article is inconsistent). Some users who do not see their product type assume it is not sold, and some leave. — [Baymard, Ecommerce Homepage UX](https://baymard.com/research-articles/e-commerce-home-page-focus)
- **[WEAK, qualitative]** Baymard examples: Boohoo's homepage focused on sale and new items, so a tester could not gauge the catalog; a tester at Nicce wrongly thought the site sold mostly men's clothing (2024 Apparel & Accessories study). One participant each. — [Baymard](https://baymard.com/research-articles/e-commerce-home-page-focus)
- **[WEAK, 2026]** Optimizely field notes, unnamed retailers, no samples: visitor-specific product recommendations raised click-through to product pages by 136% over static ones. For new visitors, static bestsellers beat static featured items by 23.01% click-through to product pages. These are click-throughs, not sales. — [Optimizely](https://www.optimizely.com/field-notes/articles/experimentation-use-cases-retail-homepage)
- **[WEAK, 2025]** Zalando launched a personalised AI discovery feed in its app in July 2025 (6 markets), expanded to 22 markets on 15 Oct 2025, limited to select iOS customers. No results published. It is an app change, not a website homepage change. — [FashionUnited](https://fashionunited.com/news/business/zalando-introduces-ai-driven-discovery-feed-in-16-more-countries/2025101568708)
- **[WEAK, 2019]** Booking tile test above (listed in section 2): fewer clicks and show-all-at-once beat carousel, per GoodUI's reading. — [GoodUI](https://goodui.org/leaks/bookings-carousel-fails-in-this-experiment-as-predicted/)
- **Not found:** Amazon, bol, Etsy, ASOS homepage experiments. A search for an Amazon hero-removal case returned only teardown or news pages with no test data. I did not verify what these sites show on their homepages today.

### Inferences
- Supported: high placement of real product and category cues helps new users judge the catalog (Baymard).
- Not supported: "top marketplaces do this because it converts better." No source shows that. Large retailers' choices may follow commercial reasons such as ad revenue (Amazon sells placement), personalisation, or brand campaigns. That is my reading, not a cited finding.
- Personalisation helps product blocks (Optimizely vendor data). A first-time B2B visitor has no history, so the "bestsellers" result is the closest analogue, and it is weak.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or named-firm A/B test of hero vs product-first homepage.
- No marketplace case study with numbers. Engineering blogs (Zalando, Etsy, bol, Booking) were searched once; nothing relevant to this question surfaced.
- Next step if needed: check Booking and Etsy engineering blogs and Baymard's paid homepage report.

## 4. What do B2B buyers do differently (repeat ordering, search-first, SKU lookup, price lists)?

### Takeaway
Plausible, but thinly evidenced. I found vendor surveys and unsourced stats, no behavioural study showing B2B buyers use search and reorder more than banners. Treat "B2B is search-first and reorder-led" as a working hypothesis.

### Cited Findings
- **[WEAK to MEDIUM, 2023 fieldwork, 2024 report]** Algolia B2B Site Search Trends 2024: 700 telephone interviews with decision-makers at B2B ecommerce companies (manufacturing and distribution; North America and EMEA), June to July 2023. These are sellers, not buyers. Algolia sells search. Claims: "Nearly all B2B purchases now start with search" (no percentage). 58% of respondents made purchases once a week or more through ecommerce portals; 33% a few times a month; 9% once a month. Online = 26% of revenue, offline 74%. 61% of online orders mix large and small value products. — [Algolia report page](https://www.algolia.com/resources/asset/report-b2b-site-search-trends-2024/)
- **[WEAK, folklore]** Algolia blog (2 Feb 2023): "92% of B2B purchases start with search," attributed to Accenture citing a Forrester blog. No sample, no year, second-hand. — [Algolia blog](https://www.algolia.com/blog/ecommerce/b2b-search-best-practices-3-ways-to-increase-the-lifetime-value-of-your-buyers/)
- **[MEDIUM, 2025]** Gartner survey of 632 B2B buyers (fieldwork Aug to Sep 2024, released 25 Jun 2025): 61% prefer a rep-free buying experience; 69% saw inconsistencies between a seller's website and what sellers say. Buyers favour digital self-service for general information and learning. It says nothing on search, reorder or banners. — [Gartner press release](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-sales-survey-finds-61-percent-of-b2b-buyers-prefer-a-rep-free-buying-experience)
- **[MEDIUM, 2026]** Baymard 2026 site search benchmark: 56% of ecommerce sites have mediocre or worse search UX. Sample not stated on the page. B2C sites. — [Baymard, Search report](https://baymard.com/blog/ecommerce-search-report-and-benchmark)
- **[WEAK, B2C]** Helloretail (Feb 2026): "30 to 50% of visitors use site search"; searchers convert 4.63% vs 2.77% for non-searchers (Econsultancy, year not given). Original sources not named in the article. B2C data. — [Helloretail](https://helloretail.com/en/blog/2026-02-24-ecommerce-search-statistics/)
- **[WEAK]** Faire (Sacra): net dollar retention above 110% (company-reported, Q3 2025, over 800K retailers). This is revenue retained from existing retailers, not a reorder share. No Faire or Ankorstore reorder percentage found. — [Sacra, Faire](https://sacra.com/research/faire)

### Inferences
- The Algolia weekly purchase data (58% weekly or more) fits a repeat-buying pattern. It comes from seller-side respondents and a search vendor, so it is a hint only.
- Gartner supports self-serve in B2B, but not any claim about banners, search or reorder.
- For a buyer who orders often, fast search, reorder and price lists likely matter more than promo space. No source tests it.

### Gaps
- No analytics study (Contentsquare, Hotjar, Baymard) splitting B2B vs B2C homepage behaviour.
- No reorder share from Faire, Ankorstore, Amazon Business, Choco or Metro found.
- NN/g B2B ecommerce research was searched and not located.

## 5. Does a sticky or thin banner (announcement bar) get noticed versus a tall hero?

### Takeaway
No reliable data. Related research says users ignore top banners that look like ads. A thin bar may be noticed only if it reads as useful site content. Unverified vendor numbers exist; do not use them.

### Cited Findings
- **[MEDIUM, 2018]** NN/g (Kara Pernice, Apr 2018) on banner blindness: users tend to ignore content that resembles ads, or sits in ad-typical spots such as the top banner or right rail. First documented 1997, replicated by eye tracking in 2007 and 2018. Example: on Apartment Therapy (26 participants) top-banner and right-rail ads got little attention. Task-relevant content styled like the site is noticed more. On phones, large inline ads can catch the eye even when seen as ads. Participant counts for several examples not stated. — [NN/g, Banner Blindness old and new findings](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/banner-blindness-old-and-new-findings/)
- **[MEDIUM, 2018]** NN/g video summary (9 Mar 2018): "People tend to ignore design elements that signal advertisements." — [NN/g video](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/banner-blindness/)
- **[WEAK, 2022]** Peer-reviewed, Tehnički glasnik 16(1), University North, Croatia: eye tracking of banner position on a news-portal wireframe. Left-position banner was noticed first; right-position banners were viewed longer. Sample size not on the abstract page. Not ecommerce, not a thin-vs-tall test. — [Hrčak](https://hrcak.srce.hr/en/271938)
- **[WEAK, unverified, DO NOT USE]** EasyApps (a vendor selling an announcement-bar app, with affiliate links) lists "72% of visitors notice the bar" and "1.4 seconds average look time" and attributes them to "NNGroup (2025)". I could not trace either to an NN/g page, and no sample is given. It also lists a 3.2% average bar CTR (VWO, 2025) with no sample. The page contradicts itself on top-performer CTR (6 to 10% vs 10 to 15%). — [EasyApps](https://easyappsecom.com/guides/shopify-announcement-bar-performance-data)

### Inferences
- A short banner does not by itself escape banner blindness. A thin bar styled like a promo in the top strip fits the pattern NN/g says users skip.
- A banner that carries task-relevant info (for example delivery, price access, minimum order) is more likely to be read than a decorative one. This follows NN/g's "task relevance" point, but no test confirms it for bars.

### Gaps
- No independent eye-tracking study of announcement bars vs hero banners.
- No NN/g 2025 announcement-bar study located.

## 6. What is the average desktop and mobile viewport in the EU in 2025 to 2026? How big is "the fold" in pixels?

### Takeaway
StatCounter measures screen size, not browser viewport. Top European desktop screen is 1920x1080 (30.86%, Sept 2026). The visible area is smaller. A working fold is about 700 to 900 px on desktop and about 650 to 750 px on a phone. These ranges are my estimates, not published figures.

### Cited Findings
- **[MEDIUM, Sept 2026]** StatCounter, desktop, Europe: 1920x1080 = 30.86%; 1536x864 = 12.5%; 2560x1440 = 5.29%; 1366x768 = 5.12%; 1280x720 = 4.24%; 1440x900 = 2.85%. Page does not state sample. — [StatCounter desktop Europe](https://gs.statcounter.com/screen-resolution-stats/desktop/europe)
- **[MEDIUM, Sept 2026]** StatCounter, mobile, Europe: 414x896 = 16.3%; 384x832 = 10.61%; 390x844 = 8.01%; 393x873 = 6%; 360x780 = 5.4%; 360x800 = 4.96%. These are screen sizes, not browser viewport. — [StatCounter mobile Europe](https://gs.statcounter.com/screen-resolution-stats/mobile/europe)
- **[WEAK]** ScreenSize.net (real sessions, self-selected audience, about 926 qualified desktop samples; the page is inconsistent between 1,080 and 751 samples): most common desktop viewport is 1920 x 920 CSS px (11%); mobile widths cluster near 400 px. Hacker News commenters questioned sample size and self-selection. — [ScreenSize.net via Zeli](https://zeli.app/story/49693226)
- **[WEAK]** Sizzy: modern iPhone CSS widths are 390 and 393 px (430 for large). It states "Viewport height changes with browser chrome, install banners, keyboards, and sticky app UI," with no height values. — [Sizzy](https://sizzy.co/blog/iphone-viewport-sizes)
- **[STRONG for the method, 2018]** NN/g counted one "screenful" as the 1080 px screen height in its 1920x1080 study. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)

### Inferences (my arithmetic, label as estimate)
- ScreenSize.net suggests about 160 px of browser and OS UI on a 1080 screen (1080 minus 920). That gives a fold near 920 px on 1920x1080.
- 1536x864 is the same 1920x1080 panel at 125% Windows scaling (common knowledge, not cited here). Subtracting about 130 CSS px for browser UI gives roughly 730 px.
- 1366x768 screens (5.12%) would leave roughly 600 px. Combined with 1280x720 these are about 9% of European desktop traffic.
- Phone viewports sit near 360 to 414 px wide. Visible height is lower than screen height because of browser bars. I have no cited number; a planning range of 650 to 750 px for 390x844-class phones is an estimate.
- Design rule that follows from the numbers: at 1536x864, a 100 px thin bar plus 72 px header leaves about 550 px for products. A 500 px hero leaves almost none.
- Sum of the six listed desktop sizes is 60.86% and of the six mobile sizes 51.28%. The rest is a long tail.

### Gaps
- No StatCounter or BrowserStack viewport (window inner-height) dataset for the EU. Google Analytics or Chrome UX data on window size not found.
- Desktop vs mobile traffic split for the EU in 2026 not collected here (relevant to a B2B storefront, where desktop share is probably higher; not verified).
- Safari and Chrome mobile visible heights not found from a primary source.

## Cross-cutting: how fast must product discovery start?

### Takeaway
No source gives a hard time limit for homepages. Evidence points to the first one to two screens: they hold about three quarters of attention (NN/g 2018) and shape whether users think the site sells their product (Baymard).

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG]** 74% of viewing time falls in the first two screenfuls. — [NN/g 2018](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)
- **[MEDIUM]** Users who cannot see their product type on the homepage may assume it is not sold, and some leave. — [Baymard](https://baymard.com/research-articles/e-commerce-home-page-focus)
- **[MEDIUM]** Mobile users rely more on the homepage to judge the site because navigation cannot keep categories visible or use hover menus. — [Baymard](https://baymard.com/research-articles/e-commerce-home-page-focus)
- **[MEDIUM, Contentsquare 2025]** Bounces rose and conversions fell 6.1% year on year across 6,000 sites. — [Contentsquare](https://go.contentsquare.com/en/benchmark-insights)

### Inferences
- "Show products in the first screen" is consistent with the evidence. "Show products instead of banners because it converts better" is not shown by any source found.

### Gaps
- Time-to-first-click and "seconds before a visitor leaves" figures (NN/g, Google/Think with Google mobile speed studies) were not retrieved. Worth one more pass.
- Mobile vs desktop scroll-depth numbers: only a 2016 Contentsquare note; nothing current.
